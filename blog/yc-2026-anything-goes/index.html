Y Combinator in 2026: anything goes?

The world’s biggest startup accelerator seems to have gone rogue, and it might be funding the wrong future.

Every few months, Y Combinator, the most influential startup accelerator in the world, picks a new batch of companies to help them take off. These batches serve as a prediction of where technology is going over the next couple of years and, therefore, where investors should be putting their money. For the rest of us mortals, we only see a piece of what may be in store.

So far in 2026, Y Combinator has funded more than 600 companies. Almost all of them use AI in some way, and for about 80%, AI is the whole product.

You don’t have to be against AI to find that number a little unsettling. The last few times people agreed on something this enthusiastically, we were talking about the dot-com bubble, NFTs, and the infamous metaverse. Many of us are concerned, and rightfully so.

And because of the power and impact of YC, it is worrying what it is choosing to reward. AI is just the surface. Here’s what it’s telling the world.

The feedback loop of AI, made with AI, sold to AI, to fix AI

A startup accelerator is supposed to be a bet on many futures, but YC seems to be clearly betting on one.

An analysis of YC’s public listings found that 78.8% of tagged companies in the 2024 batch have an AI tag, up from 20.3% in 2021. This year’s batches take it further, to the point where a company unrelated to AI is the exception, leaving all the startups that are building other things to compete for attention in a room where AI is the shiny new toy.

There are also two patterns that appear if we look a little closer. The first one is that many of these startups are simply building things for other AI systems, like sandboxes, emails, web browsers and memory, all for AI agents. They’re improving the AI agents by giving them more tools, if you will. The second pattern is that, funnily enough, some other companies in YC’s 2026 batches exist only because AI itself has created new risks. AI has made cyberattacks faster, cheaper and more frequent, so some of these startups are now protecting AI agents against prompt injection, and some sell insurance and security for agents.

It’s a never-ending loop where venture money funds AI startups, which then buy tools from other venture-funded AI startups, which sell to customers who are themselves AI companies, to fix problems that AI created. And a loop can spin for a long time, but it can’t spin forever.

Humans yes, but as a plug-in

The industry is now looking a bit like an AI ouroboros, so I believe at this point it’s fair to ask if there is a human involved anywhere anymore. And, oh, you won’t like the answer.

Yes, there are humans in YC’s 2026 batches, but some of the most memorable companies that involve them flip the usual relationship between humans and machines, in a dystopian way. Who could forget reading the description of a company that is actually a marketplace where AI agents hire humans to do tasks? Or the one of an app that connects AI agents to human experts when software gets stuck? In these products, the person is the backup, the alternative when the rest fails. The machine makes the decisions, and the human only intervenes when it reaches the limits of what it can do.

A large share of YC’s companies have also gone from building tools to help workers do their jobs to selling the job itself. In the Spring batch alone, the most common type of company was “agent as a service,” around 44% of the batch. These companies are selling a replacement for human workers, like AI recruiters, AI customer support and phone staff, even AI doctors.

Silicon Valley’s favorite answer is that this frees humans, allowing them to focus on more creative and fulfilling work, now that these tasks are automated. It’s a lovely idea, truly. It just doesn’t match what’s happening. Creative work was one of the first things replaced by AI, so these jobs are also disappearing. Working fewer hours and spending time on meaningful things and personal development sound wonderful, and I’d sign up tomorrow, but someone has to pay for that life. Nobody is thinking about what comes after human replacement, and how we’re supposed to survive. We need rules that stop AI from replacing people completely, just for safety. But we also need a universal basic income for the people it does replace. Without those two things, the promise of this amazing future means nothing.

Weapons in a startup accelerator?

Y Combinator’s famous slogan is “Make something people want.” Do people really want kamikaze drones?

Several companies in this year’s batches build weapons and surveillance systems. I really encourage you to browse the defense category on YC’s website. You’ll find several companies that sell attack drones and AI weapons. Even spy robot birds, so the “birds aren’t real” meme might not be a joke anymore.

The message this sends to investors is that weapons are a perfectly good business. How is this normal? YC is a private fund, it can decline proposals, as they do with so many companies every year. But out of thousands of applications, are you really telling me it looked at a drone designed to kill people and said yes?

I don’t expect every company to stay neutral in politics, since it isn’t always possible or even right. But I believe there is a list of industries that should be off the table, industries where the harm to people is built into the product. These industries are also excluded from many investment funds, and they are weapons, tobacco, fossil fuels, and mass surveillance.

Indirectly, if you invest in any of these as a fund or investor, you’re picking a side in someone’s war, or profiting from a product that kills millions of people every year, or helping poison the air people breathe and pushing whole communities off their land, or helping take away people’s privacy and freedom.

It is easy to pretend none of this is a problem when the consequences happen far away, sometimes years from now and to people we will never meet. But every cause has an effect on others, even the ones we can’t see. And when the most influential accelerator in the world decides those consequences are acceptable, it’s telling everyone else they are too.

So, what is YC telling us?

I won’t go into further detail about the rest of the companies and other problematic picks they’ve made, like how they’re choosing to reward vibe coding, which can eventually lead to slower apps and security holes, or how they’re funding betting, trading and prediction markets, which are problematic in themselves, or how they’re paying the companies they invest in with crypto. This is all material for another day, maybe.

But even with just what we’ve talked about here, the message is hard to miss: AI over everything, humans only if strictly necessary, weapons and bets are fair game, and speed over care. That’s what the world’s most influential accelerator is choosing to reward this year.

I’m not totally against AI, or against technology changing how we work. But we need to be very careful with the tools we create. A screwdriver helps your hands with a task, but you’re still the one making the decisions, and if you use it to hurt someone you face the consequences. Many of the tools being built today work differently. They make decisions on their own, at a scale no one can really predict, and some of them are designed to harm. We’re building things we can’t completely control faster than we’re building the rules to make sure they’re used as they should be, and if they aren’t, to hold actual humans accountable.

Right now, a lot of the investors in Silicon Valley are putting millions on these tools, and YC’s 2026 batches are the clearest proof. But only a handful of people are building those screwdrivers. And, if these batches are truly a preview of the next few years, shouldn’t we be asking if we want to live in that future?

I have a lot of respect for founders, and for what Y Combinator has built over the years. I worked at a company from the YC Summer 2016 batch, and I saw firsthand what that support can do for a business. No one is asking them to stop doing so.

The ask, from me and people who share these thoughts, is simple: for YC to take action on where its funding is going. That could mean committing to a list of industries it won’t fund, the way many investment funds already do, and making it public, or dedicating more of its funding to solving the problems some of these companies are making worse. Neither of these would cost YC its identity, but both would send a very different message. Trust is hard to rebuild, but this would be a good place to start.
